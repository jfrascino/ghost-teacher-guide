About the film

GHOST retells all ten chapters of the novel in Castle "Ghost" Cranshaw's own voice, in a street-art collage style. It pauses five times for short Track Breaks that each end on a discussion question.

29:28running time
10 + 5chapters + Track Breaks
17chapter markers in the video
CCcaptions on screen throughout
  • Use it after reading, or chapter by chapter. Each film chapter matches a book chapter and opens with its title card, so you can stop at any card.
  • Pause at every "Pause & Discuss" card. The question stays on screen for a few seconds; pause there for discussion.
  • Captions are on screen the whole time. Words light up as they're spoken, which supports struggling readers and English learners.
  • It's a retelling, not a reading. The book is under copyright, so the dialogue and narration are new wording that follows the book's events. Only one line is quoted directly: "I got a lot of scream inside" (Chapter 3). The film is no substitute for the novel. Use it to review, discuss and spark interest.
  • Facts were checked against the novel and two published teaching guides (the Simon & Schuster reading group guide and the Words Alive curriculum). Where sources disagreed, the novel won.

The five Track Breaks

Each Track Break is under a minute and ends on a "Pause & Discuss" card. Pause the video when the card appears.

Track Break 1 · Running Away vs. Running Toward (7:10, question at 7:51)

Ghost says he never had to practice running. He just knew how. What does it mean that his greatest talent was born on his worst night?
  • The book's cover asks whether Ghost is running for his life or from it. Every run in the book can be sorted on that line.
  • Reynolds has said he chose track because "running" means many things: running away from something, toward something.
  • Have students keep the running-away / running-toward scorecard for the rest of the film.

Track Break 2 · Seeds & Shoes (12:31, question at 13:12)

Why does Ghost cut up his own shoes, instead of asking for help?
  • Sunflower seeds: his father ate them shell and all; Ghost cracks them one at a time and keeps the good part.
  • Reynolds has explained the seeds as the adults in a kid's life: some chew you up, some only take the salt, some take time to crack the shell. Students sort the book's adults into those groups.
  • Shoes as identity: no-name high-tops, cut-down low-tops, the stolen silver shoes, then the same silver shoes paid for. Each pair is a different version of Ghost.

Track Break 3 · Who's Got Ghost's Back? (17:19, question at 18:00)

Who is the adult in your corner? And what did they do that made you trust them?
  • Mr. Charles (shelter, the record book, "world's greatest"), Coach (rides home, homework checks, won't let him quit), Ma (work, nursing classes, his favorite dinner), Principal Marshall (could have suspended him and didn't).
  • Ghost's father is gone, but Ghost isn't alone.
  • Personal question: invite answers in writing first. Not every student will want to share aloud.

Track Break 4 · Two Men, One Block (24:46, question at 25:19)

Was Coach's punishment fair? What would you have done if you were Coach?
  • Coach mirrors Ghost: same neighborhood, fathers who hurt them, both runners.
  • The consequences: no uniform, sitting out, old shoes, cleaning the cab, apologizing to Tia face to face. Coach doesn't call the police and doesn't tell Ma.
  • Then Coach pays. Debate: is that a reward, or a debt Ghost now has to live up to?

Track Break 5 · So Who Wins? (28:11, final question at 28:52)

You can't run away from who you are. So what are you running toward?
  • The book ends as the gun fires. We never learn who wins.
  • The film frames it as two gunshots: one that made Ghost run away, one that tells him to run toward something. (That pairing is a common classroom reading, not a statement from the author.)
  • Writing extension: an alternate ending, or the first page of Patina from Patty's point of view.

Themes, symbols & craft

The central theme is running away versus running toward. Coach states it in Chapter 9: you can't run away from who you are, but you can run toward who you want to be.

Craft

  • First-person voice: Ghost tells his own story with humor, slang and asides, and it still hits hard. Notice how jokes sit right next to painful moments.
  • Open ending: the book stops on the gun. Discuss why an author would leave out the result.

Activities

One viewing (30 minutes) plus a 40–50 minute discussion fits two class periods. These activities fit around it.

Before viewing

  • Quick-write (5 min): "What's something you've run away from? Something you've run toward?" Students keep it private.
  • Hand out the run scorecard.

During viewing: the run scorecard

Students mark each run as away or toward and note what Ghost is feeling. Open the printable version.

After viewing

  • Seed sort: list the adults in the book. Who "chews you up," who "only takes the salt," who "cracks the shell"? Defend each placement with a scene.
  • Fair or not? Four corners on Coach's punishment (strongly agree to strongly disagree), then a one-paragraph argument.
  • Write the next 30 seconds: the book ends on the gun. Students write the race, or write it from Lu's, Ma's or Brandon's point of view.
  • Book vs. film: name two moments the film shortened or left out (see Book vs. film) and explain what's lost.
  • My world record: echoing the chapter titles, students title a chapter of their own life "World Record for..."

Sensitive content

The book opens with a father shooting at his wife and son. Preview Chapter 1 (0:19–3:48) before showing it, give students a heads-up, and offer an alternative for anyone affected by gun or domestic violence.
ContentHow the film handles it
Gun / domestic violence (Ch. 1, 10)Told in narration with silhouettes: the father is a faceless shadow, the gunshot is a white flash and a muffled sound. No blood, no one hurt on screen. The starter's pistol at the end is shown as a starter's pistol.
Alcohol and drug addictionGhost's father's drinking is stated in one line. Coach's father's addiction, the medal sold for drugs and his death are told by Coach, not shown. The drug dealer at the basketball court (Ch. 7) is left out.
Bullying and fighting (Ch. 3)Brandon's insults are kept. The fight is a stylized burst of milk, a tray and motion lines, with no injuries.
Theft and lying (Ch. 5–9)Shown plainly, with the book's full consequences.
Poverty, albinism, disabilityKept as in the book. Lu's albinism and Patty's mother's amputations are named respectfully.
Ma's movie (Ch. 6)The R-rated film Ma watches isn't named.

Tips from the published guides

  • Make personal sharing optional. Let students write about a character instead of themselves.
  • Tell the counselor before you teach Chapter 1.
  • Don't treat Glass Manor as a bad place: it's also where Coach and Mr. Charles come from.
  • Treat the blunt way Ghost first describes Lu as a flaw to examine, not a model.

Book vs. film

The film keeps every major event in order but compresses about 180 pages into 30 minutes. All dialogue is reworded. Moments it shortens or leaves out make good "what did the film miss?" prompts:

  • Ch. 2: Coach's promise that track will have Ghost dunking by next year.
  • Ch. 3: the names of Ghost's friends (Dre and Red) and the gym teacher "Big Perm."
  • Ch. 4: Aaron sharing his water bottle, and Ghost running shirtless with his jeans rolled.
  • Ch. 5: Mr. Charles's family of "geniuses" (doctor brother, professor sister, lawyer parents), only touched on.
  • Ch. 6: the flashback of the dog chasing Ghost home from the court.
  • Ch. 7: the weekend with Aunt Sophie and King, and the afternoon at the basketball court.
  • Ch. 9: fartlek training and the weekly practice schedule. Curron's exact words are new; the book says only that he mocked Sunny's mother.
  • Ch. 10: the relay and hurdles races before the 100, and Coach giving Ghost his uniform the night before.

Facts the film keeps exactly: Coach's medal is gold; Ghost's sprint times are 12.5 then 14 seconds; the shoes are size 9, from Everything Sports; Ghost runs in lane 6, Lu in lane 1, Brandon in lane 8 for the Bolts. The book never names Coach's Olympic event, and neither does the film.
